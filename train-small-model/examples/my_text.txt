Garden journal, written by hand and typed up later.

Monday. The morning was cold and the soil was still dark from the rain. I planted two rows of peas along the north fence and pushed thin sticks into the ground beside them so the vines have something to climb. The robin watched me the whole time from the gate. When I left, it dropped down and pulled a worm out of the fresh row.

Tuesday. Wind from the west all day. The tomato seedlings on the windowsill are leaning toward the glass, so I turned the tray around. A seedling grows toward the light, and if you never turn it, the stem grows long and weak. Turning the tray every morning keeps the stems short and strong.

Wednesday. I mixed compost into the bed by the shed. Good compost smells like a forest floor after rain. Bad compost smells sour, which means it is too wet and has no air. The pile behind the shed was sour, so I turned it with the fork and added dry leaves. Dry leaves bring air, and air brings the small creatures that do the real work.

Thursday. Rain in the morning, sun in the afternoon. The peas have not come up yet, but it is too early to worry. Peas take about ten days in cold soil. I weeded the onion bed and found three slugs under the board by the path. I moved the board to the far corner of the garden, slugs and all.

Friday. The first pea shoots are up, pale and bent like small hooks. In two days they will straighten and turn green. I watered the seedlings on the windowsill and gave the basil a little more room by moving it to its own pot. Basil does not like cold feet, so it stays inside until the nights are warm.

Saturday. My neighbour came over with a bag of garlic cloves from her own garden. She told me to plant each clove pointed end up, two fingers deep, and a hand apart. Garlic planted in the cold comes up strong in the spring. We planted the whole bag together along the south edge of the bed and talked about the weather, the price of seeds, and her cat that sleeps in her lettuce.

Sunday. A quiet day. I sat on the bench and drew a map of the garden in my notebook: peas on the north fence, onions by the path, garlic on the south edge, compost behind the shed, and the empty bed by the gate where the tomatoes will go when the nights are warm. A map helps me remember what I planted and where, because by the middle of summer everything looks like one green wave.

Monday. Frost on the grass at dawn. I covered the young peas with an old sheet the night before, and they came through fine. The sheet was white with frost on top and dry underneath. A thin cover keeps in the warmth of the soil, and the soil gives off warmth all night.

Tuesday. The robin is building a nest in the hedge by the gate. It carries dry grass and small roots in its beak, back and forth, all morning. I will leave the hedge alone until the young birds have flown. Birds eat the insects that eat the plants, so a nest in the garden is good news.

Wednesday. I sowed carrot seed in a shallow line and covered it with fine soil. Carrot seed is tiny and slow, and the soil must stay damp until the seedlings come up, which can take three weeks. I laid a board over the row to keep the soil damp. I must remember to lift the board as soon as the first green shows, or the seedlings will grow pale and weak under it.

Thursday. The tomato seedlings now have their first true leaves. The first two leaves on a seedling are seed leaves and look nothing like the plant. The true leaves come next and look like small tomato leaves, with the same smell when you touch them. I moved each seedling into a bigger pot and buried the stem a little deeper. A tomato grows new roots along any part of the stem that is under the soil.

Friday. Rain all day. I stayed inside and cleaned the tools: the fork, the spade, the trowel and the small knife. A clean tool lasts longer and does not carry disease from one bed to the next. I wiped the blades with oil and hung them on the wall of the shed in the same order as always, so I can find them in the dark.

Saturday. Sun at last. The peas are a hand tall and have found the sticks. I tied the strongest vines loosely with soft string. The onions are standing up straight and the garlic is showing green tips. The carrot row is still bare, so the board stays on. My neighbour waved from her garden. Her cat was asleep in the lettuce again.

Sunday. I walked around the garden with the map and checked every bed. Peas growing, onions growing, garlic up, carrots not yet, tomatoes waiting inside, compost warm and sweet. I wrote the date on the map and put it back in the notebook. Next week, if the nights stay warm, the tomatoes go out to the bed by the gate.
